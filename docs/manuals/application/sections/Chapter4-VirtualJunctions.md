@page application_manual_ch4_virtual_junctions Chapter 4: Virtual Junctions on a Surveyed Trunk

## Problem

A network built from survey or GIS data arrives as hundreds of short
segments. The segments are not separate pipes — they are one pipe,
described piecewise, with a point every time the survey recorded one.
Modelling each break as a junction invents a manhole that is not there:
a node with storage, a place for water to pond, an entrance and exit
loss, and one more equation to solve.

A **virtual junction** is the alternative: a splice with no storage that
transmits momentum, so the solver sees one continuous conduit through it.
This chapter builds a trunk out of 370 of them and asks what the routing
choice does to the answer.

## Deck

Decks: `docs/figures/decks/cedar_glen/cedar_glen_fv.inp`,
`cedar_glen_dw.inp`, `cedar_glen_dw_extran.inp`

Cedar Glen to FRS, a synthetic raw-water conveyance generated by
`generate_model.py` in the same folder: 387 conduits, of which 370 are
joined by virtual junctions and 17 by real ones, a 25 900 ft trunk of
72, 78 and 90 in pipe with two lateral branches, gentle slopes of
0.10 to 0.30 %, and a flow-regulating basin with a service orifice and an
elevated overflow.

The interesting geometry is in the middle: a **double-pocket reach**
between the two tie-ins, where the invert falls into a pocket at 442 ft,
rises over a 1.55 % adverse riser to a crest at 455 ft, falls into a
second pocket, and lifts again. That reach pressurises at each surge
peak, which is the point of the model — a valved-conveyance grade is
where routing choices stop agreeing.

The forcing is a 24-hour double surge-and-drain hydrograph, the second
peak at 85 % of the first. Every coordinate, elevation and name is
generated from a seeded script; nothing comes from any client data.

## Options that select the formulation

The three decks differ only in their routing options. The splices are
the same in all three:

```
[VIRTUAL_JUNCTIONS]
;;Name             Elev       Rim
TU0000124            467.6278    11.0000
TU0000243            467.2712    11.0000
...
```

and the routing is

```
FLOW_ROUTING         FV          ; cedar_glen_fv.inp
FLOW_ROUTING         DYNWAVE     ; cedar_glen_dw.inp, with SURCHARGE_METHOD SLOT
FLOW_ROUTING         DYNWAVE     ; cedar_glen_dw_extran.inp, with EXTRAN
```

A virtual junction is subject to the rules of its kind: exactly two
conduits, the same cross-section on both, zero offsets. Under the
finite-volume router the splice is transparent by construction — the
cells either side are neighbours across a face. Under dynamic wave it is
a node the solver is told not to give storage to.

| Deck | Flow-routing continuity error | Run time |
|---|---|---|
| `cedar_glen_fv.inp` | 0.000 % | 102 s |
| `cedar_glen_dw.inp` (slot) | **−8.285 %** | 29 s |
| `cedar_glen_dw_extran.inp` | −0.026 % | 9 s |

*Table 4-1 What each routing cost, and what it conserved*

**The grade line runs through the splices.** Figure 4-1 is 260 trunk
nodes plotted at one instant, and there is no step at any of them. That
is the whole claim of a virtual junction, and it is worth checking on a
real model by eye: a sawtooth in a profile like this one is the signature
of accidental junctions with storage.

**Two of the three routings agree; one does not.** The finite-volume and
EXTRAN runs sit on top of each other over most of the trunk, both
pressurising the flat reach to about 457 ft. The Preissmann-slot run
disagrees, and Figure 4-2 shows how: at the node 25 800 ft along, near
the basin, it oscillates between 420 and 455 ft for hours at a time while
the other two hold a smooth recession.

**Continuity says which to believe.** The slot run's flow-routing
continuity error is −8.285 %: it lost more than one part in twelve of the
water it was given. The finite-volume run's is 0.000 % and EXTRAN's is
−0.026 %. On this network the slot's surcharge accounting is the problem,
not the virtual junctions — and the error is large enough that no
calibration of the deck would rescue it.

**The cost.** 102 seconds against 29 and 9, for a 387-conduit model over
24 hours. The finite-volume router pays for resolving the pressurisation
front in the pocket reach; local time stepping is what keeps that from
being far worse, since the trunk's short survey segments would otherwise
put the whole model on the shortest cell's clock
(@ref hydraulics_ref_ch8_lts "Hydraulics 8, §8.5.6").

**What to take from this.** On a conveyance like this one — long, gently
sloped, pressurising in pockets — the choice that matters most is not
whether to use virtual junctions, which is settled by how the network was
surveyed, but the surcharge accounting. Read the continuity error first,
then the profile, then the cost.
